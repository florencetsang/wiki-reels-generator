## Use Wikipedia's Vital Articles as your backbone

Before building your own field tree, know that Wikipedia editors already maintain a curated, hierarchical, importance-ranked list of topics called **Vital Articles**, organized into exactly the tiered structure you're describing:

- **Level 1** — 10 articles (the most fundamental topics)
- **Level 2** — 100 articles
- **Level 3** — 1,000 articles
- **Level 4** — 10,000 articles
- **Level 5** — ~50,000 articles

Each level is subdivided by field (History, Science, Arts, etc.), and larger fields naturally get more articles — which is precisely your "more for History, fewer for Early Civilization" instinct, already done by humans. **Level 4 (~10,000 articles) is an almost ideal launch library**, and it lines up exactly with the 10k figure from our earlier cost estimate. These lists live on structured wiki pages (e.g. `Wikipedia:Vital articles/Level/4/History`) that you can fetch and parse directly.

## Can you actually get "top N per field"? Yes — but define "top"

"Top" means two very different things, and you probably want both:

**Top by importance** (curated) — this is what Vital Articles and WikiProject assessments give you. Every article can carry a WikiProject *importance* rating (Top / High / Mid / Low) and a *quality* rating (FA, GA, B, C, Start, Stub). This is editor-judged significance, great for guaranteeing your feed isn't full of obscure stubs.

**Top by popularity** (data-driven) — this is pageviews, i.e. what people actually read. For a reel app optimizing engagement, this is arguably the better ranking signal.

## The relevant APIs

Here's the toolkit, and how they combine:

**MediaWiki API — `categorymembers`.** Lists pages in a given category. This is your field/category enumerator:
```
https://en.wikipedia.org/w/api.php?action=query&list=categorymembers
   &cmtitle=Category:Physics&cmlimit=500&format=json
```
Big caveat: **Wikipedia categories are a messy directed graph, not a clean tree.** They nest deeply, contain cycles, and mix "is-a" with loosely-related associations. A naïve recursive descent from `Category:Science` will eventually drag in half the encyclopedia. You must cap traversal depth and filter.

**PetScan** (`petscan.wmcloud.org`) — the tool built exactly for this problem. It traverses a category tree **to a depth you specify**, lets you intersect/subtract categories (e.g. "in Physics but not in Physics stubs"), and can filter by page size or link count. This is the pragmatic way to enumerate a clean candidate set per field.

**Pageviews REST API** — ranks candidates by real readership. Two useful endpoints:
```
# Views for a specific article over time
https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/metrics/pageviews/per-article/
   en.wikipedia/all-access/all-agents/{Article}/monthly/{start}/{end}

# The globally most-viewed articles for a day/month
https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/metrics/pageviews/top/
   en.wikipedia/all-access/{year}/{month}/{day}
```

**PagePileAnalysis / Quality data (ORES / Lift Wing)** — machine-predicted article quality scores, useful to auto-filter out stubs if you don't want to rely on manual WikiProject ratings.

**The Vital Articles pages themselves** — since they're wiki pages, you fetch them via the standard content API and parse the article links out of the structured lists. This gives you the importance-tiered, field-organized backbone for free.

## The pipeline I'd actually build

Combine them rather than picking one:

1. **Seed** each field/subfield in your tree with the corresponding **Vital Articles Level 4** section — guarantees quality and importance, and it's already field-partitioned with sensible size weighting.
2. **Expand** larger fields beyond Vital Articles using **PetScan** to enumerate the category (bounded depth), giving you a bigger candidate pool.
3. **Rank** each field's candidates by **Pageviews API** monthly views, and **filter out** anything below a quality threshold (via ORES or WikiProject rating) to kill stubs.
4. **Take top N per field**, where N is set by your per-field weighting above.

This gives you exactly what you asked: a per-field top-N list, biased toward things people genuinely read, floored by editorial quality, and sized proportionally to each field.

One efficiency win: the pageviews data is also available as **downloadable dumps**, so if you're scoring tens of thousands of candidates you should batch-process a dump rather than making tens of thousands of live API calls — kinder to the API and much faster.
